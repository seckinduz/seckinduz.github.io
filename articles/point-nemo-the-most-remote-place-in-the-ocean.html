Point Nemo: The Most Remote Spot in the Ocean

Resumen en español: El punto del océano más alejado de cualquier tierra, a unos 2.700 kilómetros de la costa más cercana. Las agencias espaciales dirigen hacia esta zona naves retiradas, apodada el cementerio de naves espaciales. Consejo: No se puede visitar sin más. Síguelo en un mapa y lee sobre las misiones espaciales que terminan allí.

In the middle of the South Pacific Ocean there is a spot that is farther from any land than any other point on the sea. Known as Point Nemo, it lies roughly 2,700 kilometers from the nearest coastlines. The closest land consists of tiny, remote islands and the shores of Antarctica. It is called the oceanic pole of inaccessibility, and it is often described as the loneliest place on Earth.

The name comes from Captain Nemo, the fictional submarine commander created by Jules Verne, and it also happens to mean nobody in Latin.

How it was found

The location was calculated in the early 1990s by a surveying engineer who used a computer program to find the point on the ocean surface that is farthest from all land. The result was a spot at about 48 degrees 52 minutes south and 123 degrees 23 minutes west. Because it is based on calculations from coastline data, small differences in the map used can shift the exact coordinates a little.

The area is usually equidistant from three surrounding points of land: a tiny island in the Pitcairn group to the north, an island in the Easter Island region to the northeast and the Antarctic coast to the south.

A graveyard for spacecraft

Point Nemo is known for another reason. Space agencies use the surrounding region as a safe disposal area for large spacecraft at the end of their lives. When an old satellite or station is brought down in a controlled way, the debris that survives reentry is steered to fall in this remote stretch of ocean, far from ships, aircraft and people.

More than two hundred and fifty spacecraft are believed to rest on the seabed there, including the Russian space station Mir, which was brought down in 2001. The International Space Station is planned to follow at the end of its service life, in a controlled descent expected in the early 2030s.

An ocean with little life

The water around Point Nemo is part of the South Pacific Gyre, a huge rotating system of currents. Nutrients are scarce, so the surface waters hold little life compared with coastal areas. That is one more reason the region is considered suitable for disposal: there is less marine life affected by falling debris.

The nearest humans are often not on the water at all. Astronauts on the International Space Station pass overhead at an altitude of roughly 400 kilometers, which can be closer than any person on land.

Can you visit?

Reaching Point Nemo requires a long and costly expedition by ship, and there is nothing to see except open water. Most people explore it through maps, documentaries and books about space exploration. A globe and a good atlas are all you need to understand how far away it is.

A few expeditions and sailing races have crossed the area, and it is possible to follow positions online when they do.

Why Point Nemo matters

Point Nemo is a place defined by absence. It is a reminder of how large the oceans are and how rarely humans reach their middle. At the same time, it shows how human technology is shaping even the most remote corners of the planet. It is both a symbol of isolation and a practical solution to one of the challenges of the space age.
