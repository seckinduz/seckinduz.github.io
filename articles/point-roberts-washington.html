Point Roberts: The American Town You Can Only Drive To Through Canada

Point Roberts is a small community at the tip of a peninsula south of Vancouver, British Columbia. It belongs to the state of Washington, but it is attached to the Canadian mainland, not to the rest of the United States. To reach it by land from the American side, you must drive through Canada, cross the border into Point Roberts and, when you leave, cross the border again. It is one of the few places in the United States that works as a true exclave.

The town is quiet, with beaches, forests, small shops and a close-knit community. Its location, however, makes everyday life a lesson in how borders shape ordinary decisions.

The line on the map

The story begins with the 49th parallel. In 1846 the United States and Britain signed a treaty that set the boundary in the Pacific Northwest along that line of latitude. The agreement was meant to be simple: everything south of the line would belong to the United States and everything north of it to Britain.

The peninsula of Point Roberts, however, sticks out just south of the parallel, and when the line was drawn, no one thought carefully about its shape. The tip of the peninsula therefore stayed American even though it is surrounded by water on three sides and by Canadian land on the fourth. The name comes from the English explorer George Vancouver, who named the point in 1792 after a friend, Henry Roberts.

How people live there

Only a small number of people live in Point Roberts year-round, but the population grows in the summer when seasonal residents and visitors arrive. Many property owners are Canadians who keep vacation homes there. The community has a small airport, a marina, a few restaurants and shops and a loyal group of volunteers who run local services.

Children who attend school beyond the local level often travel through Canada by bus, passing two border checkpoints each way. Adults cross for groceries, health care and work. Cross-border traffic is part of the daily routine, and residents plan their time around wait times at the crossing. Fuel and groceries often attract Canadian shoppers, which supports the local economy.

When the border closed

The special geography became front-page news during the coronavirus pandemic. When the border restricted non-essential travel in 2020, many Canadians could no longer visit their homes and businesses in Point Roberts, and the town's economy suffered. Residents faced longer trips for supplies, and some businesses struggled to survive. When restrictions eased, the community slowly recovered, and the experience left a strong memory of how dependent the town is on open borders.

Visiting Point Roberts

Bring passports for everyone and check border wait times before you go, especially on summer weekends. The beaches are good for walking and watching sunsets, and on clear days you can see the mountains across the water. A paper road map, a picnic blanket and a flexible schedule make the trip more relaxing. Remember that rules for entering both countries can change, so confirm the latest requirements in advance.

Why Point Roberts matters

Point Roberts is a living example of how a single line on a map can create a surprising reality. It reminds us that geography and politics do not always match, and that communities can adapt to unusual situations with patience and humor. It is also a great reminder that the best stories on the map are often found in the smallest places.
