Dallol: Ethiopia's Colorful and Extreme Volcanic Landscape

Resumen en español: Una zona volcánica en la depresión de Danakil donde piscinas ácidas y formaciones de sal pintan el suelo de amarillo, verde y naranja. Es uno de los lugares más calurosos de la Tierra. Consejo: Visitalo solo con una excursión organizada y guías locales. La seguridad y el calor extremo hacen poco aconsejable viajar por libre.

Dallol is a volcanic area in the Danakil Depression in northeastern Ethiopia, in the Afar region. It is famous for its vivid landscape of yellow, green, orange and white, created by minerals, salt and hot springs. The surface looks like another planet, and scientists study the area for clues about life in extreme environments.

It is also one of the hottest inhabited regions on Earth in terms of average temperature, and one of the lowest places on land, lying well below sea level.

How the colors are formed

The Danakil Depression sits where tectonic plates are slowly pulling apart. Hot underground water rises through the crust, bringing minerals and gases to the surface. As the water evaporates in the intense heat, it leaves layers of salt and sulfur.

The colors come from different compounds. Yellow comes from sulfur, green from certain iron salts and acidic liquids, and white from salt. The pools can be highly acidic, and the air can carry strong smells of gas. The landscape changes over time as pools dry out, form crusts or appear in new places.

Life in the heat

Temperatures in the depression often climb above forty degrees Celsius and sometimes much higher. Despite the harsh conditions, the Afar people have lived in the region for generations. For centuries they have mined salt from the nearby salt flats and transported it by camel caravan.

Researchers have been studying the acid pools for signs of microscopic life. The idea that organisms might survive in such extreme conditions makes the area interesting for scientists who work on the question of life beyond Earth.

Travel and safety

Visiting Dallol is not simple. The region is remote and extremely hot, and security conditions in northern Ethiopia can change quickly. Independent travel is not recommended. If you plan a trip, use an organized tour with local guides and check the latest official travel advice before booking.

A long-sleeved sun shirt, a wide-brim hat, plenty of water and electrolytes are essential. Stay on marked paths, because the crust can be thin and some pools are dangerous.

Planning and preparation

Anyone who studies Dallol quickly learns that preparation matters more than equipment. The best time to travel in the region is during the cooler months, when daytime temperatures are still very high but slightly more bearable. Organized expeditions usually include armed escorts, camp equipment and drivers who know the terrain. Photographers should protect cameras from salt dust and heat, and carry spare batteries, since charging points are rare. Respecting local customs, asking before photographing people and leaving no trash are basic rules that help keep the area open for responsible visitors.

Why Dallol is unforgettable

Dallol looks more like a painting than a landscape. It shows the creative power of geology and the resilience of life. It also reminds travelers of the importance of preparation, respect for local communities and caution in extreme environments. For many people, a photograph of Dallol is the closest they will come, and it is still one of the most astonishing images of our planet.
