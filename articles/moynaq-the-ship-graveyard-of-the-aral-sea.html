Moynaq: The Port That Lost Its Sea

Resumen en español: Un antiguo puerto del mar de Aral. El agua se retiró durante décadas tras el desvío de los ríos y hoy hay barcos oxidados en el desierto, lejos de cualquier orilla. Consejo: Planifica una excursión guiada por tierra desde Nukus y lleva agua y protección solar.

In the far northwest of Uzbekistan, in the region of Karakalpakstan, there is a town called Moynaq. Once it was a busy fishing port on the Aral Sea. Today it lies tens of kilometers away from the water, and rusting ships stand on dry ground like monuments to a vanished sea. It is one of the most striking images of environmental change in the world.

The sight of heavy vessels in the middle of a desert is both haunting and thought-provoking.

The shrinking of the Aral Sea

The Aral Sea was once one of the largest lakes in the world. It was fed by two big rivers, the Amu Darya and the Syr Darya. In the twentieth century the Soviet Union built large irrigation projects to grow cotton and other crops in Central Asia. Water from the rivers was diverted into canals, and far less reached the sea.

From the 1960s the sea began to shrink. Over the decades the water level fell dramatically, the shoreline retreated and the lake split into smaller parts. Salinity rose, fish died and the fishing industry collapsed. Moynaq lost its main source of work, and many residents moved away.

A landscape of rust and dust

As the seabed dried, wind carried salt and dust across the region, creating health problems for people nearby. The climate became harsher, with hotter summers and colder winters. The exposed seabed became a new desert, sometimes called the Aralkum.

The ship graveyard is now the best-known site in Moynaq. Visitors walk among old fishing vessels and look at the empty horizon where the water used to be. A small museum and memorial tell the story of the town.

Signs of hope

There has been a partial recovery in the northern part of the Aral Sea, known as the Small Aral. A dam built in the mid-2000s in Kazakhstan helped keep water in that section, and fish have returned, supporting local fishing. The southern part, closer to Moynaq, has been largely lost, and bringing it back is not considered realistic.

Efforts continue to improve irrigation efficiency, plant vegetation on the dry seabed to reduce dust and support local communities.

Visiting Moynaq

A guided overland trip from Nukus is the usual way to visit. The journey is long and conditions can be hot and dusty, so bring plenty of water, sun protection, a wide-brim hat and a headscarf or mask for dust. Check travel advice and ask a local operator about road conditions.

Visit with respect for the local people, who live with the consequences of the disaster every day.

Lessons from the Aral Sea

Scientists often use the Aral Sea as a case study in environmental management. The disaster shows how changes to a river system can transform climate, health, economy and culture across a whole region. It also shows that people can respond: fishing communities in the northern part have benefited from restoration work, and international organizations have supported projects for cleaner water and healthier soil. Visiting Moynaq helps people understand these lessons through experience, not statistics, and encourages a thoughtful look at how much water we really use for the food and clothes we buy.

What Moynaq teaches

Moynaq is a powerful lesson about the effect of human decisions on the environment. A project designed to produce crops transformed an entire region. The story encourages careful planning, wise use of water and awareness of long-term consequences. For many travelers it becomes one of the most memorable and sobering places they visit.
