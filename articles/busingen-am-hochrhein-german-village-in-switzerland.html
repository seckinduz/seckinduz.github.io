Büsingen am Hochrhein: The German Village Inside Switzerland

On the north bank of the Rhine, close to the Swiss city of Schaffhausen, lies a village that is officially part of Germany but is surrounded on every side by Switzerland. Büsingen am Hochrhein is a German exclave of just a few square kilometers and roughly one and a half thousand residents. It is one of the clearest examples of how everyday life can work smoothly in a place that looks impossible on paper.

People in Büsingen use both euros and Swiss francs, shop across the border as a matter of routine and share many services with their Swiss neighbors. At the same time they are German citizens in a German municipality.

A border shaped by old territories

The village's strange situation comes from centuries of changing territories in the Rhine region. Local rulers, empires and cantons traded rights and land, and the line that eventually separated Switzerland and Germany left Büsingen on the German side while the area around it ended up Swiss.

Locals have often told the story of a vote held in 1918, when a large majority of residents reportedly voted to join Switzerland. The idea failed because there was no suitable swap of territory that both countries could accept. Büsingen therefore stayed German, and the unusual arrangement continues today.

How everyday life works

The most surprising part of the arrangement is how practical it has become. Büsingen is inside the Swiss customs area, which means that goods move between the village and Switzerland without ordinary customs checks. A special treaty between the two countries sets out how taxes, postal services, schools and transport are handled.

The village has two postal codes, one German and one Swiss, and its telephone numbers use the Swiss system. Cars carry a unique registration prefix that includes the letters BÜS, which makes the village one of the few places in Germany with its own special plate. Many workers commute into Swiss towns every day, and the Swiss franc has a strong role in wages and prices.

To get from Büsingen to the rest of Germany, you have to cross Swiss land. There are well-known paths and roads that make this routine, and the border area is open and peaceful.

Visiting Büsingen

Büsingen is a quiet destination, and most visitors come from Schaffhausen or from the nearby Rhine Falls, one of the largest waterfalls in Europe. You can combine the two in a single day. A boat ride on the Rhine is a relaxed way to see the landscape, and there are walking paths along the river and through the countryside.

Do not expect large monuments. The appeal is in the ordinary details: signs, license plates, shops and cafés that reveal the double identity of the place. If you enjoy collecting small border stories, this is a rewarding stop.

Why Büsingen is worth knowing

The village proves that a border does not have to cause conflict. Büsingen is an example of cooperation, shaped by patient agreements and local pragmatism. It also shows how identity can be flexible: residents are German by law, Swiss by daily habit and local by choice. For travelers and map lovers, it is a gentle reminder that the most interesting borders are often the ones that work so well that few people ever notice them.
