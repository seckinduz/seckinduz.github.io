Kinderdijk: 19 Windmills That Drained a Polder

Resumen en español: Diecinueve molinos construidos hacia 1740 para drenar el polder bajo. Hoy las estaciones de bombeo modernas hacen el trabajo pesado y los molinos son Patrimonio Mundial de la UNESCO. Consejo: Alquila una bici y recorre los caminos del dique. El amanecer y el atardecer son los momentos más tranquilos.

Kinderdijk is a village in the Netherlands, east of Rotterdam, famous for its row of nineteen historic windmills. They stand along the water channels of the Alblasserwaard polder and form one of the most recognizable landscapes in the country. In 1997 the site was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list as an outstanding example of Dutch water management.

Today the mills are quiet monuments, but for centuries they worked day and night to keep the land dry enough for farming and living.

A landscape created to fight water

A large part of the Netherlands lies below sea level, and the Alblasserwaard is one of the low-lying areas where rivers and tides always threatened the land. The mills at Kinderdijk were built around 1740 to pump water from the polder into a higher reservoir basin and then into the river, using the power of the wind.

The mills worked in a chain. One mill lifted water to a basin, a second lifted it higher, and so on until it could flow out. This system required teamwork between millers and local water boards, which were among the first democratic institutions in the country.

The legend of the cradle

The name Kinderdijk means child dike. A well-known legend tells of a huge flood in 1421, called the Saint Elizabeth's flood, after which a cradle with a baby was seen floating in the water. A cat was said to keep the cradle balanced by jumping from side to side so that it would not tip over. The baby survived and was found by people who gave the place its name.

No one knows how much of the story is true, but it has become a beloved part of the local identity and appears in books, paintings and souvenirs.

From wind to engines

Windmills did a good job, but they depended on the weather. In the nineteenth century, steam engines began to support the work, and later diesel and electric pumping stations took over most of the heavy tasks. These stations are still used today to keep the polder safe.

The windmills were kept as a heritage site, and some of them are still lived in. Visitors can enter a few of the mills to see how the millers and their families used to live, with small rooms, wooden beams and large wheels. On special days, the sails turn and the mills operate as they did in the past.

Visiting Kinderdijk

The easiest way to explore the site is by bicycle or on foot along the dike paths. You can also take a boat tour on the canals. Sunrise and early evening are the quietest times, and the light on the water is especially beautiful. Bring a light jacket, a camera and a refillable bottle, and be ready for a bit of wind.

There is a visitor center with exhibits and information, and tickets for the museum mills are available at the entrance. Check seasonal opening times before you go. Allow at least half a day so that you can walk the whole path, visit one of the museum mills and stop for coffee in the village.

Why Kinderdijk matters

Kinderdijk is a monument to cooperation and ingenuity. The nineteen mills show how a community can work together to protect itself from water, and the landscape is a living reminder of how much of the Netherlands was shaped by human effort. For travelers, it is a peaceful and scenic place that tells a big story.
