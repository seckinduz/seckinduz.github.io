Gibraltar: The Rock, the Runway and the Apes

Resumen en español: Una roca a la entrada del Mediterráneo donde una carretera principal cruza la pista del aeropuerto y los macacos de Berbería mandan en la parte alta. Es un territorio británico de ultramar junto a España. Consejo: Sube pronto en teleférico. Guarda la comida: los macacos saben lo que es una bolsa.

Gibraltar is a small British Overseas Territory at the southern tip of Spain, where the Mediterranean meets the Atlantic. Its most famous feature is the Rock of Gibraltar, a huge limestone ridge that rises about 426 meters above the sea. The territory has fewer than forty thousand residents and covers a bit under seven square kilometers, yet it is one of the best-known places in Europe.

It is also a place of quirky details: a road that crosses an airport runway, wild monkeys on the hillside and a mix of British, Spanish and Mediterranean traditions.

How Gibraltar became British

The Rock has been a strategic site since antiquity, and ancient writers linked it with the Pillars of Hercules. In the early eighteenth century, during the War of the Spanish Succession, an Anglo-Dutch force captured Gibraltar. The Treaty of Utrecht in 1713 formally handed it to Britain, and it has remained British since then.

Spain has long disputed the arrangement and raised questions about the territory's status. Residents have repeatedly voted to remain British, and the situation has been affected by discussions around Brexit and new agreements between Britain, Spain and the European Union.

The runway and the macaques

One of Gibraltar's most unusual sights is its airport. The main road into the town, Winston Churchill Avenue, crosses the runway, and traffic is stopped by barriers whenever a plane lands or takes off. Pedestrians and drivers wait for planes the way people elsewhere wait for trains.

The upper Rock is home to the Barbary macaques, the only wild monkey population in Europe. They have become a symbol of the territory and one of its biggest attractions. Visitors are advised not to feed them and to keep bags closed, because the monkeys are smart and quick to take an opportunity.

Visiting Gibraltar

Most visitors cross from the Spanish town of La Línea, usually on foot. You will go through passport control, then walk across the runway into the town. The cable car takes you up to the top of the Rock, where there are viewpoints, caves and the macaques.

The main street has shops, pubs and cafés, and prices are in pounds, although euros are widely accepted. Take a light jacket for the wind on the Rock and keep food out of sight. A day is enough to see the highlights, but you can add more time for the tunnels, the nature reserve and the dolphin boat trips in the bay.

Practical tips for a day trip

A day in Gibraltar is easy to plan. Most visitors park in Spain and walk across the border, because queues for cars can be long. Carry a passport or ID card, and check current entry rules before you go. The cable car, the Great Siege Tunnels and the Upper Rock Nature Reserve are the main attractions, and combined tickets are available. Wear comfortable shoes because paths are steep, and remember that the wind can change quickly at the top. Late afternoon light gives beautiful views across the bay toward Africa.

Why Gibraltar stands out

Gibraltar is a small place with big stories. It combines strategic history, an unusual border and a sense of local identity that feels both British and Mediterranean. For anyone exploring strange borders and hidden places, it shows how a tiny territory can carry enormous symbolism and still be a welcoming place to visit.
