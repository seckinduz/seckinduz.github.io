Bir Tawil: The Desert No Country Wants

Resumen en español: Unos 2.000 kilómetros cuadrados de desierto que ni Egipto ni Sudán reclaman. Dos líneas fronterizas distintas, trazadas en 1899 y 1902, hacen que a cada país le convenga reclamar la otra franja, así que esta no pertenece a ningún Estado. Consejo: Casi inalcanzable: no hay servicios y el acceso requiere permisos. Lo sensato es viajar desde el sillón.

Bir Tawil is a rocky desert area of roughly 2,060 square kilometers on the border between Egypt and Sudan. It has no permanent population and no recognized government, and it is one of the few places on Earth where no country claims the land. The reason is not a lack of interest in territory but a legal puzzle created by two different border lines drawn more than a century ago.

It is a remote region of sand, rock and scattered wells, used mostly by nomadic groups who move through the area with their animals.

Two borders, one puzzle

In 1899, an agreement between Britain and Egypt set the boundary between Egypt and Sudan along the 22nd parallel north. A few years later, in 1902, the British administration drew a new administrative boundary that followed the traditional use of land by local tribes. This second line placed a large triangle in the east under Sudanese administration and Bir Tawil under Egyptian administration.

Both lines continue to exist in the way each country sees the map. Egypt prefers the 1899 line, which gives it Bir Tawil but leaves the Hala'ib Triangle on the Sudanese side. Sudan prefers the 1902 line, which gives it Hala'ib but leaves Bir Tawil to Egypt.

Why nobody claims it

The Hala'ib Triangle is much larger than Bir Tawil, around ten times the size, and it has a Red Sea coastline and economic value. Each country is more interested in the bigger prize. If Egypt were to claim Bir Tawil under the 1902 line, it would weaken its argument for the 1899 line and could lose Hala'ib. Sudan faces the same problem in reverse.

As a result, both governments avoid claiming Bir Tawil, and the land remains a rare example of terra nullius, land belonging to no one. Over the years a few individuals have publicly announced symbolic claims, including a widely reported attempt to declare a kingdom there, but none of these has been accepted by any state.

Can you visit?

Bir Tawil is very difficult to reach. There are no facilities, no roads for ordinary travel and no official services. Access requires permits and experienced local support, and the region can be affected by security issues. For most readers, armchair travel is the sensible way to explore it.

A good atlas, a map poster and a documentary about the Sahara will give you plenty of context. Satellite maps also let you see the rocky terrain and the surrounding desert.

Reading the maps

To understand Bir Tawil, it helps to look at a map with both boundary lines marked. The straight 1899 line runs along a parallel, while the 1902 line bends to follow local tribal land use. The two lines cross, and the areas between them create two odd shapes: the large Hala'ib Triangle and the small Bir Tawil trapezoid. Many online maps show the trapezoid in a neutral color to indicate that it is unclaimed. Some mapmakers still assign it to one country or the other, which is why you may see different versions in atlases and apps.

Why Bir Tawil fascinates people

Bir Tawil is a perfect example of how legal logic can create a result that seems almost absurd. It is land that everyone could take but nobody wants, because accepting it would mean giving up something more valuable. That paradox has made it one of the most discussed places on the internet and a favorite example in geography classes.
