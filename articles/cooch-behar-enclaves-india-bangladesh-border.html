The Cooch Behar Enclaves: Life Inside Enclaves Within Enclaves

For decades, the border between India and Bangladesh in the Cooch Behar region was one of the most complicated in the world. Hundreds of tiny pieces of land belonging to one country were surrounded by the territory of the other. Some of those pieces even contained smaller enclaves of the first country, creating enclaves inside enclaves. Residents lived in places that were officially part of one state but could be reached only by crossing the territory of another.

In 2015, India and Bangladesh finally exchanged most of these territories and gave residents the option to choose their citizenship, closing a chapter that had lasted for generations.

How the enclaves appeared

The roots of the pattern go back to the agreements and land arrangements of the eighteenth century between local rulers in the region. Estates and territories were organized in ways that did not follow a simple map. When the subcontinent was partitioned in 1947, the new border followed administrative lines that cut through this complicated pattern and left isolated pieces on the wrong side.

For decades the enclaves existed in a legal gap. Residents often struggled to get basic services such as schooling, electricity, health care and legal documents. Police and government officials could not easily reach them, and travel between the enclaves and the rest of their own country was difficult.

The 2015 exchange

Both governments worked for years on an agreement to settle the question. In 2015 they exchanged 162 enclaves: 111 formerly Indian enclaves were transferred to Bangladesh and 51 formerly Bangladeshi enclaves were transferred to India. Tens of thousands of residents were affected.

People were allowed to choose whether to stay where they were and become citizens of the country that now administered their land, or to move to the other country. Most chose to stay, and the exchange was widely described as a historic step that ended decades of uncertainty.

A visit with respect

The region is not a theme park. It is a lived-in landscape of farms and villages, and the people there have often endured long periods of difficulty. Anyone interested in seeing the area should do so with a local guide, respect for private property and sensitivity to the people who live there.

A light, breathable outfit, a water bottle with a filter and a small notebook are practical companions. Because the situation, access rules and local services can change, it is wise to check current conditions and use reliable regional information before making travel plans.

What happened after the exchange

After the 2015 exchange, people in the former enclaves began receiving services that had been out of reach for decades. Schools, electricity connections and identity documents slowly became available, although progress was uneven and some residents still faced difficulties. Many families who had lived without official records for generations were finally able to register births, marriages and land. The experience continues to be studied by researchers and by officials working on other border disputes, because it shows both the benefits of a negotiated settlement and the effort needed to make it work in practice.

What the story teaches

The Cooch Behar enclaves are an example of how fragile borders can be and how important cooperation is in solving them. They show that a map line can shape lives for generations, and that a negotiated solution can bring real improvements to people who spent years in legal limbo. For geography fans, this is one of the most inspiring border stories of the last decade.
